Likhoeli tsa Selemo

The twelve months
and what they name

Every month in the Basotho year is named for something happening in the world that month — a plant sending up shoots, a grasshopper arriving in swarms, grain hardening past the reach of birds. Each entry below gives the derivation as it is attested in the sources, not as it is guessed at.

A calendar kept by the moon

Letsatsi · Sun Lefatše · Earth Khoeli · Moon

Turning at the ratio they keep in life: the moon rounds the earth about twelve and a third times while the earth rounds the sun once. That third is why the year will not sit still.

The Basotho calendar is lunisolar: its months follow the cycles of the moon's phases, fitted to the solar farming year. Because the moon does not keep step with the sun, the new year arrives at a different time each year.

Three phases are named as the year turns. When the moon goes dark, when it first shows a sliver, and when that sliver widens — that last sight is the signal that a new year has dawned.

Selemo sa Basotho, the Basotho New Year, is marked on the first of August, in the month of Phato. It was traditionally welcomed with Mokete oa leoa, a thanksgiving to the forebears and to God for the harvest. It is still marked today with gatherings, music, the Mohobelo and Serobolelo dances, horse riding, food and dress.

Two seasons, and four

Sesotho speakers traditionally recognise two seasons rather than four. The four-season words exist and are used, but the older division is the one the farming year turns on.

SeasonCoversRoot

Selemo is the same word as the word for year, and comes from -lema, to plant. The year and the planting are not separable.

Deep Sesotho

These month names belong to what is called Sesotho sa boholo — deep Sesotho. They are not much used in casual speech, where the English months have taken over, but they hold their place in news broadcasts and other formal media, and they remain more common there than the borrowed names.

The words themselves are old. Mariha reconstructs to Proto-Bantu *-tîka, cold weather or cold season; Lehoetla to *-ginja. These are concepts carried across hundreds of Bantu languages, older than the language that holds them now.
